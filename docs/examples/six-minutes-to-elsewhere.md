# Six Minutes to Elsewhere

A fictional activity produced in a fresh Explore case, lightly edited for publication. The schedule and worked playthrough are inspectable; six-minute timing and enjoyment have not been tested with human players.

**For:** exactly 3 adults · **Time:** 6 minutes · **Aim:** make one imaginary place together. No acting, artistic skill, or personal disclosure.

**Materials:** one sheet of paper and three pencils. Put the paper where everyone can reach it. One player is the **Reader**; they also draw. Take turns clockwise as **First, Second, Third**. Use a station clock if one is visible; otherwise keep each drawing turn to roughly 40 seconds.

## Read and play

**0:00–0:30 — Welcome.** Reader says: *“We have six minutes to draw one impossible place together. There are no good or bad drawings, and nobody has to perform or explain anything. Everything is invented. Once a mark is on the page it becomes true, though a later choice may change it.”*

**0:30–0:50 — Begin.** Reader says: *“Draw a small circle in the middle and write ARRIVAL inside it. We go clockwise: First, Second, Third, then once more.”*

**0:50–1:30 — First.** *“Anywhere away from ARRIVAL, draw one impossible landmark. Give it a name of one to four words.”*

**1:30–2:10 — Second.** *“Draw a route from ARRIVAL toward the landmark. Take it **through** the landmark or **around** it; your pencil makes the choice. If you go through, visibly change the landmark and add one word saying how. If you go around, put one protected thing inside the loop and name it.”*

**2:10–2:50 — Third.** *“Continue the route to a second landmark. If the route went through, draw what that change awakened. If it went around, draw what the detour protected. Name the new landmark.”*

**2:50–3:30 — First.** *“Split the route into two branches. Put a little roof at one end for **shelter** and an eye at the other for a **view**. Make one branch easy with a plain line and the other difficult with a zigzag. You choose which is which.”*

**3:30–4:10 — Second.** *“Choose one branch by extending it. If you choose shelter, draw something from outside the map moving closer. If you choose the view, reveal something that has been inside the map all along. Name what appears.”*

**4:10–4:50 — Third.** *“Change the whole place: either bridge two features that were separate, or bar one route that was open. Draw your choice. Then write a title for the entire place across the top.”*

**4:50–5:30 — Everyone.** *“At the same time, add one tiny trace left by what just happened: perhaps footprints, ripples, smoke, birds, lights, or a mark of your own. If a bridge was built, put your trace on both sides of it. If a route was barred, let your trace gather at the barrier.”*

**5:30–6:00 — Finish.** Reader says: *“Pencils down. I’ll trace with one finger from ARRIVAL as far as our routes now allow, obeying every change we made. We made…”* Reader traces the route, then reads the place’s title. Keep the sheet as the record of your place.

## Worked example: one whole playthrough

The group draws **ARRIVAL**. First draws an upside-down waterfall and names it **UPFALL**. Second takes the route *through* it, turns its falling lines into rings, and writes **RINGING**. Because that change must affect the next turn, Third draws what the ringing awakened: an orchard of bell-shaped trees named **BELL ORCHARD**.

First splits the route. The plain, easy branch leads to the eye; the zigzag, difficult branch leads to the roof. Second chooses the easy **view** branch, so must reveal something that was inside the map all along: the outline of a huge sleeping fish beneath every earlier feature, named **GLASSBACK**.

Third chooses to build a bridge from BELL ORCHARD to the viewpoint above GLASSBACK, then titles the whole place **THE COUNTRY ON A SLEEPING FISH**. Because a bridge was built, all three add traces on both sides: tiny footprints crossing it, ripples below it, and birds above it. The Reader traces from ARRIVAL, through the changed UPFALL, past BELL ORCHARD, across the new bridge, and ends at the view of GLASSBACK: the final shape exists because each earlier choice changed what the next person could make.
