# Aligning materials

Use this reference when sources appear to agree or disagree but may be answering different questions, when the amount of independent evidence matters, or when a finding has to be carried into a new context. The examples below are constructed illustrations, not evidence about real projects.

## Check that the sources describe the same object

Before comparing results, establish what their objects and conditions share and where they differ. Different conditions may be the reason for comparison, but they cannot be silently treated as equivalent. Line up:

- the definition or metric each source uses for the key term;
- the population, dataset, or artifact it was measured on;
- the version, date, or configuration;
- the conditions and the range over which it claims to apply.

Much apparent conflict dissolves here. "Two studies disagree about whether X helps" often means one measured beginners on short tasks and the other measured experts over months. That is not a disagreement about X; it is two findings with different reach.

Example: a benchmark report showing 82% and a paper showing 61% on "the same" task may use different test-set versions. Neither contradicts the other until the versions are matched, so report the version rather than the gap.

## Trace what each source actually depends on

Apparent breadth is not the same as independent evidence, but a shared label is not enough to dismiss a source either. A shared author group, lab, or dataset does not by itself collapse two results into one: the same data can support a genuinely new analysis, and the same group can run a second, separate measurement. Trace the real dependence rather than counting files.

Ask what each added source rests on:

- **Restatement:** it reports the same observation again, adding reach but not new evidence.
- **Re-analysis:** it asks a new question or applies a new method to the same data, and can contribute something the original did not.
- **Replication:** it produced a new measurement, possibly on a different population, and can confirm or contradict the first.

Say which one you have. Ten articles repeating one trial are one measurement; two papers from one group that ran two separate studies may be two. Judge what the observations establish through their relevance, methods, limitations, and actual dependence, rather than counting documents or measurements into a confidence score. A re-analysis can contribute even when it shares the original data.

## Keep source fact, inference, and transfer apart

Three different claims are easy to blur:

- **Source fact:** what this material establishes in its own setting, such as an effect measured in a named population.
- **Inference:** what follows from that fact by reasoning, such as expecting the same mechanism to behave the same way here.
- **Transfer:** what would have to be true for the finding to hold in the current task, and what carrying it over would cost.

State which one you are making. A source can be trustworthy and still not transfer, because the mechanism may depend on conditions the current context lacks. Naming the transfer conditions is more useful than either adopting the finding wholesale or dismissing it.

Example: a study showing that spaced practice helps students pass a course does not by itself establish that a two-week onboarding flow should add it. The source fact is solid; the transfer depends on whether the audience returns often enough for spacing to mean anything.
