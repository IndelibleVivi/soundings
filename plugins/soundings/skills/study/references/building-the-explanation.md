# Building the explanation

Use this reference when the materials are more than a list to sort: when several findings could share one mechanism, or when two readings compete for the same evidence. The examples below are constructed illustrations of the method, not findings about real projects.

## Propose a mechanism, not just a pattern

A pile of findings becomes understanding when something explains why they hold together. State the relationship in a sentence you could test, as in "people abandon this because progress is invisible until the end" or "the effect comes from the pause, not the reward". An explanation that only restates the data in longer words adds nothing.

When the evidence allows more than one reading, keep the real competitors. The reading where the effect comes from the pause and the one where it comes from the reward predict different outcomes when you remove one part, so they are not the same claim wearing two labels.

## Test it against what it should explain

Put the candidate explanation to work and watch where it breaks:

- what does it explain that a rival does not?
- which case it should cover does it fail to cover?
- which counterexample, outlier, or negative result does it leave standing?
- what happens under a changed condition, a different population, or the opposite setup?

The aim is not to defend the first explanation but to find the one that covers the most material with the fewest special cases.

## A short illustration

Three accounts of a tool disagree: two say it helped, one says it did not. Simply sorting them, "mostly positive, one negative", reports the disagreement and adds little. If the positive accounts concern repetitive work and the other concerns tasks needing judgment, task structure becomes a candidate explanation for the difference. That suggests comparing how much repetition the current project contains and whether the same mechanism is present. The accounts do not yet establish causation, nor does “did not help” establish harm; training, implementation quality, or task difficulty could also explain the pattern. The synthesis adds a useful conditional hypothesis and a way to distinguish it from rivals.

## Keep it honest

If no explanation survives the material, say which readings were ruled out and what evidence would decide between those that remain. A bounded "we still cannot separate X from Y, and here is the test" is a result. Do not force a unity the evidence does not support, and do not present a preferred explanation as more settled than it is.
