# Coverage and limits

Use this reference when deciding how far to gather, when the available material is partial, or when a conclusion could be read as stronger than the coverage supports. Any examples below are constructed illustrations, not findings about real projects.

## Gather to the understanding the task needs

Study does not require a fixed number of sources or a complete literature, and it does not stop merely because a decision is already supported. When making sense of the material is itself the result, that depth is the goal: keep gathering while new material still changes the explanation, and stop when it no longer does. When the task needs only a bounded judgment, enough evidence to carry that judgment is a fine place to stop. The two stopping points are different, and neither should be mistaken for the other.

Read-only is not a reason to withhold verification. A read-only request can still allow searching, reading, and comparing; what it withholds is acting on the result. When a precise, decision-relevant gap could be filled by looking outward and the request permits it, look, and bring the result back. When the user has bounded the study to the material they supplied, or the gap is broad enough that closing it would change the task, name the gap instead of quietly expanding scope.

## Name what is unread or unreachable

State the important material you could not open or check: an inaccessible study, an unread appendix, a version that is gone, or a source that only quotes another. Do not let "not found" become "does not exist", and do not let an inaccessible piece disappear silently from the conclusion.

A bounded statement, such as "this holds across the three datasets we could reach; the vendor's internal set is not available", is more useful than an unqualified claim that will not survive the first missing case.

## Hold the conclusion to its evidence

Keep the reach of the conclusion equal to the coverage. Distinguish what the material establishes from what is likely, plausible, or still open, and where a key case is missing prefer an explicit unknown over a confident sentence that hides it.

Avoid manufacturing completeness: a tidy structure, a comparison table, or a confident summary can make partial coverage look settled. The structure should follow what the material can support, not the other way around.
