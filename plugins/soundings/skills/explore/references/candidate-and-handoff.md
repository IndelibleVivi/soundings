# Candidate and handoff

Use this reference when producing the candidate, when a result feels weak, or when deciding how far Explore's responsibility runs. Read it when an experience is thin or has been rejected, before another candidate is committed. Any examples below are constructed illustrations, not findings about real projects.

## Make something that can be met

The candidate should be concrete enough that the person reacts to the thing itself, not to a description of it. Depending on the work, that may be a playable slice, a scene, a script, a rule set, a rendered sample, or a run-through. Match the fidelity to the judgment: too thin, and the person has to imagine away exactly what was in question.

Produce it inside the current authorization. If making the candidate is already permitted and fits the task, build it now rather than proposing to build it later.

## Tell the idea's failure from the execution's

When a result does not land, inspect the actual experience and ask which part failed:

- **Mechanism failure:** the relationship itself does not produce the intended effect, because the loop is not fun, the pacing never releases, or the choice does not matter. Changing the surface will not fix this.
- **Untested execution:** the relationship may be fine, but the build is too rough to have tested it, through placeholder content, missing feedback, or an unreadable first pass. Polish a little and try again before rejecting the mechanism.
- **Interpretation failure:** the candidate serves a plausible but different experience from the one requested. More fidelity will not help until the intended use scene is recovered.
- **Candidate-fidelity failure:** the fragment omitted the condition the person needed to judge, so its rejection does not yet establish that the relationship failed.

Do not discard a good relationship because a rough draft hid it, and do not polish a broken one until it merely looks finished. Check the actual timed sequence, interaction, state change, or export at realistic conditions when those carry the effect. Compare the failed result with the intent and any reference relationship rather than inferring a universal preference from one rejection. For a complete constructed diagnosis, read [proactive inquiry examples](../../search/references/proactive-inquiry-examples.md#constructed-example-repeated-rejection).

Make the next candidate discriminate among the remaining explanations. Preserve accepted goals, content, controls, and relationships unless the observation gives a reason to change them. An attractive alternative can still be wrong for this scene if its preconditions or state ownership conflict with the accepted experience.

## Control the comparison, not the whole work

When you compare two candidates to learn which difference caused an effect, change one condition and hold the rest steady, so a reaction tells you what did it. That discipline belongs to a deliberate comparison, not to the work itself: a whole scene, experience, or product can be developed as an integrated thing, and you are not required to isolate single variables while making it.

## Keep accepted goals, and continue into production

Goals the person already accepted stay accepted; ordinary uncertainty about details is not a reason to reopen them or to seek new approval. Hand back the candidate with what it shows, what it does not yet show, and what it makes possible next. Judge it by returning to the original use scene: what can the person now perceive, choose, or do that the previous version did not allow? When a specialized production method is available to build it out, the task continues there within the same authorization rather than returning unfinished work to the user for a fresh approval.
